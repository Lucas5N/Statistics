Numbers

Each cell shows the value measured on the m attackers, with the theoretical value in brackets. The Chebyshev bound is σ²/(nε²). The last column is the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance between the empirical distribution of (Sn − nμ)/(σ√n) and N(0,1). It cannot go to zero faster than about 1/√n, because Sn lives on a lattice.

Interpretation

Read the n systems as the steps of one attacker's walk and the m attackers as m independent walks. Each attacker scores Xi on system i: in the Bernoulli model 1 if the system is penetrated and 0 otherwise, in the Rademacher model +1 or −1. Sn is the aggregate score of one attacker, and the m values of Sn are m independent samples of its distribution.

The size n changes the law of the score. Var(Sn) = nσ² grows, so the absolute spread grows like √n. Var(Sn/n) = σ²/n shrinks, so the success rate of every attacker concentrates around μ (weak law of large numbers, by Chebyshev P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²)). Var(Sn/√n) = σ² stays constant, and in the Rademacher case (μ = 0) the distribution of Sn/√n approaches N(0,1) (central limit theorem). With μ ≠ 0 the mean of Sn/√n is √n·μ and diverges, so only the centered and scaled version converges.

The number of attackers m does not change the law of Sn. It only controls how well the histograms and the sample variances estimate it, with an error of order 1/√m, and how many rare high-scoring attackers appear: the expected number of attackers above a threshold t is m·P(Sn > t).
