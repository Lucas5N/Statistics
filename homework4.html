Welford's recurrences and sampling statistics

Ex. 1: Welford's recurrence formulas

Let x̄k = (1/k)·Σi≤k xi be the mean of the first k values, SSk = Σi≤k (xi − x̄k)² the sum of squares, and s²k = SSk/(k−1) the sample variance (k ≥ 2). Put δk = xk − x̄k−1.

Mean (Knuth)

k·x̄k = (k−1)·x̄k−1 + xk  ⇒  x̄k = x̄k−1 + δk/k.
Hence x̄k − x̄k−1 = δk/k and xk − x̄k = δk·(k−1)/k.

Sum of squares

Since SSk = Σ xi² − k·x̄k², subtracting SSk−1 leaves
SSk − SSk−1 = xk² − k·x̄k² + (k−1)·x̄k−1².
From the Knuth step, (k−1)·x̄k−1 = k·x̄k − xk, so (k−1)·x̄k−1² = (k·x̄k − xk)·x̄k−1. Therefore
SSk − SSk−1 = xk(xk − x̄k−1) − k·x̄k(x̄k − x̄k−1) = xk·δk − x̄k·δk.
SSk = SSk−1 + (xk − x̄k−1)(xk − x̄k) = SSk−1 + ((k−1)/k)·δk², with SS1 = 0.

Variance

Substituting SSk = (k−1)·s²k and SSk−1 = (k−2)·s²k−1:
s²k = ((k−2)/(k−1))·s²k−1 + δk²/k for k ≥ 2, so s²2 = δ2²/2.

The recurrence adds only terms of the same size as the data, while Σx² − k·x̄² subtracts two large, nearly equal numbers and loses precision when the mean is large compared with the spread.

Code

function welford(data) {
  let k = 0, mean = 0, ss = 0;
  for (const x of data) {
    k++;
    const d = x - mean;      // delta_k = x_k - mean_{k-1}
    mean += d / k;           // Knuth:   mean_k
    ss   += d * (x - mean);  // Welford: SS_k = SS_{k-1} + (x_k - mean_{k-1})(x_k - mean_k)
  }
  return { mean, ss, variance: ss / (k - 1) };
}

Worked example

Why not the one-pass sums formula

The formula s² = (Σx² − (Σx)²/n)/(n−1) is algebraically identical, but it subtracts two huge, almost equal numbers. With a large mean and a small spread, the rounding error of the doubles is bigger than the quantity being computed.

Ex. 2: Sampling mean and variance

Each of m hackers attacks n systems and gets a score in {0, …, 10} on each one, drawn from a fixed distribution. For every hacker, Welford gives x̄ and s² of the n scores, and everything is then averaged over the m hackers. A single trajectory of 10000 scores per hacker is used, and the statistics are read off after the first n scores, for each n in the table.

Probability mass function of the score of one attempt.

Each cell shows the value measured over the m hackers, with the theoretical value in brackets. For the sample variance, Var(s²) = (μ₄ − ((n−3)/(n−1))·σ⁴)/n, where μ₄ is the fourth central moment of the distribution. The column avg of SS/n is the variance estimator with divisor n, whose expected value is ((n−1)/n)·σ². The last column is the fraction of hackers whose interval x̄ ± 1.96·s/√n contains μ.

Conclusions

The average of the sample means is close to μ and the average of the sample variances is close to σ², for every n, including n = 10. The estimators are unbiased: E[x̄] = μ and E[s²] = σ². The divisor n−1 matters for this. Dividing SS by n would give ((n−1)/n)·σ² on average, a bias that is visible for small n. The avg of SS/n column shows that bias: it sits below σ² by the factor (n−1)/n. The remaining gaps are Monte Carlo error and shrink as m grows. The last column shows the interval x̄ ± 1.96·s/√n covering μ in about 95% of hackers. It is a little lower for small n, because 1.96 is the normal quantile and s itself is noisy, and it approaches 0.95 as n grows.

This is what makes sample statistics useful in inference: μ and σ² are unknown in practice, but x̄ and s² are computed from the data alone and are correct on average. Since Var(x̄) = σ²/n, the standard error of the mean can be estimated by s/√n, and this is what confidence intervals and tests are built on. Welford makes the computation online and numerically stable.

Increasing n does not change the averages, which stay at μ and σ², but it reduces their variability. The standard deviation of the sample means falls as σ/√n, so 100 times more systems give a 10 times tighter estimate. The standard deviation of the sample variances also falls as 1/√n, but with a larger constant, √(μ₄ − σ⁴). Estimating the variance needs a fourth moment, so it is less precise than estimating the mean at the same n.
